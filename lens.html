A recap: population, measurement and estimation

This chapter is a short recap of the three components of an epidemiological study: population, measurement and estimation. You will have met parts of them in term 1, spread across several modules. Here they sit together, as a lens for looking at any study. It is meant to be short. The Components page, the last chapter, has the detail and the try-it widgets. Read it only if you want more.

1.1 · Population

Population: the people you observe are not automatically the people you mean

PopulationWho is the answer about, and whose data do we have?

Every study starts with its population. As you may remember from term 1, it comes in steps, each inside the one before.

The target population is who the answer is meant to be about, by person, place and time: say, all adult wizards in the UK. It is the goal in mind, and it can rarely be listed. There is no way to reach everyone. So a study defines a source population that it can list and count, such as the staff register of one school, Hogwarts. From that list it samples people. Those sampled who agree to take part are the study population. Those still there, with a valid reading, are the analyzed sample.

People fall out at every step. When who falls out depends on what is being measured, the answer tilts. This is selection bias, and it can tilt a single prevalence as well as a comparison. The steps can also stop short of the target. A school’s staff are working adults, so a study of them answers first for them.

Why it matters here: each design gets its people in its own way. That entry rule decides whom its answer can stand for. Chapter 3 takes the designs in turn. Section C.1 has more, and a school to sample.

Other words. Many texts split this into internal validity (does the answer hold for the people the study drew from?) and external validity (does it carry to the target?). For a single number such as a prevalence, the split helps less, because a gap at any edge can bias it. That is why some authors speak of target validity (Westreich et al. 2019).

Population

Who gets in?

The entry rule: everyone present on one day, everyone free of the outcome at a start date, people picked because they have the outcome, or volunteers. Each design in chapter 3 has its own.

Population

Who is still there when the study looks?

People die, move away or stop taking part, before entry or after it. Whatever happens to them next leaves with them.

Population

Whom do they stand for?

The answer is about a target population. A prevalence must stand for it. A comparison needs, above all, groups that are alike (Rothman, Gallacher & Hatch 2013). Ask how the people studied could differ, and whether that matters for this question.

Check yourself. A study invites everyone on the school’s staff register, but people with diabetes are less likely to reply. Which question does this touch, and would inviting twice as many people fix it?
One way to think about it

Who gets in. The invitation was fair, but the people who answered are not. So the prevalence comes out too low. Inviting twice as many repeats the same pattern, with a narrower interval around the wrong value. Writing again to people who did not reply, and comparing those who replied with the register, would help. Try it in Figure C.1.

1.2 · Measurement

Measurement: a definition says what you mean; a tool tries to capture it

MeasurementHow were the data obtained, and do they represent what we intend?

Measurement is about the data: how were they obtained, and do they represent what we meant? A definition says what you mean, such as who truly has diabetes, or who drinks daily. A test, a question or a record tries to capture it.

Two words come with it. A measure is valid if it matches the true value, and reliable if measuring again gives the same answer. When measurement errs, people land in the wrong group: information bias, or misclassification.

Error can enter at three places, and each can harm validity, reliability or both (Hulley et al. 2013):

  • the characteristic itself: fasting glucose varies through the day. Even a good meter in careful hands reads differently at 8 a.m. and after lunch, so when it is measured matters;
  • the instrument: a meter that is not calibrated, or a question that leads;
  • the observer: who reads, who asks and who writes it down.

For glucose, the observer matters little, because the meter shows a number. It matters more when the measure is a judgment. Rating depression in an interview has no meter. The interviewer asks, decides how far to probe and scores the answers. Two interviewers can rate the same person differently. And an interviewer who knows the person has diabetes may probe harder. That is why studies train observers and follow a fixed script. Where they can, they also keep the observer unaware of who is in which group. Section C.2 lets you try a test.

Why it matters here: you can choose the best tool, train the people who measure and fix a protocol only when the study collects its own data as things happen. A study that uses records made earlier, for another purpose, takes the measurement as it was made, by whoever made it. So the design sets a limit on how good the measurement can be. That does not make looking back always worse. Records written before anyone knew the outcome cannot be colored by it. And a well-kept register can be very good. Chapter 2 names this timing, and chapter 3 shows what each design can control.

Measurement

How is each thing defined, and what measures it?

Everything a study uses needs a definition and a tool. That includes the exposure, the outcome, and other factors that could explain a difference. The tool may be a question, a test or a record. Some measures are numbers, like glucose. A cut-off turns a number into a case, and loses detail near the line. Near a cut-off, more readings per person steady the answer. More people do not.

Measurement

When is each thing measured?

Exposure before the outcome, both at the same visit, or the exposure looked back on once the outcome is known. The order decides what the outcome can color.

Measurement

Who decides, and who knows?

Your own protocol, or records made for another purpose. And does the person measuring know who is in which group? Measure everyone the same way, and keep the measurer unaware where you can.

Check yourself. A survey counts everyone with one fasting glucose of 126 or more. Name one error a bigger sample would not fix, and one design choice that would.
One way to think about it

A single reading near the cut-off can land on either side by chance. Near a cut-off, that random error pushes the count one way, and studying more people repeats it. Averaging two or three readings per person shrinks it. A meter that reads high needs a better test. No number of repeats fixes it. Section C.2 lets you try both.

1.3 · Estimation

Estimation: a count, a prevalence, a risk and a rate answer different questions

EstimationWhat number, exactly, and compared with what?

Estimation is about the number. Which summary answers the question, among whom, and over what time? If groups are compared, compared with whom? The same people can give very different numbers, each the right answer to a different question.

Figure 1.3 follows twelve invented Hogwarts staff from 2016 to 2026. On survey day in 2026, 3 have diabetes: a count, the burden a clinic must carry. They are 3 of the 11 still there: a prevalence of 27%, how common it is now. Of the 11 free of diabetes in 2016, 2 developed it: a risk of 18% over ten years, if the one who moved away stayed free. The same 2 cases over the 95 person-years for which the 11 were watched give a rate of about 2 per 100 a year. The rate counts the mover’s five years and no more.

Answering with the wrong number answers a different question. Chance sits beside every number. It gives an interval, and the interval says nothing about who was missed. Section C.3 sets the numbers side by side in Figure C.5.

Why it matters here: not every design gives every number. A cohort that follows people from a start can give all four. A survey on one day gives a count and a prevalence, but no new cases, so no risk or rate. A case-control study starts from people who already have the outcome, so on its own it gives an odds ratio, not a prevalence, a risk or a rate. The design decides which numbers are on the table before anyone calculates one. Chapter 3 takes each design in turn.

Estimation

Which number?

A count for burden, a prevalence for the share who have it now, a risk or a rate for new cases, an odds when a study starts from the outcome. Pick the one the question asks for.

Estimation

Compared with whom?

Groups formed by people’s own habits, by their outcome, or by randomization. How the groups formed decides what a difference between them can mean.

Estimation

What about what you couldn’t see?

Chance gives an interval. People who were lost give a range. Neither fixes the other, so report both.

Check yourself. Two groups are compared. What must be the same in both, and what does a comparison of groups that formed by people's own habits tell you?
One way to think about it

The same quantity, over the same period, from the same kind of start. Groups that formed themselves give an association. On its own, it describes. Reading it as a cause needs more (section 3.5, and Research Methodology). More in section C.3.

1.4 · What goes wrong

Each component has its own way to go wrong

RecapSelection, information, a different question; chance alongside

Write the target first, then compare what the study did with the ideal its goal calls for. This is the tool to carry into the workshop and into chapters 2 to 5. Each component asks one question, and each has its own way of going wrong. Together, anchored in time, the three define the estimand: the exact quantity a study is after.

Population

Who is the answer about, and whose data do we have?

What goes wrong

Selection bias

The people analyzed differ from the target in ways linked to what is measured: the source, who is selected, who agrees, whose data are complete.

Measurement

How were the data obtained, and do they represent what we intend?

What goes wrong

Information bias

The measure is not valid (wrong the same way each time) or not reliable (different on a repeat), through the observer, the instrument or the characteristic. Error that differs between compared groups can create a difference.

Estimation

What number, exactly, and compared with what?

What goes wrong

A different question answered

The number is not the quantity you meant: the wrong denominator or period, departures mishandled, or a crude number read as a standardized one.

ChanceRuns alongside all three. A bigger sample shrinks it and fixes none of them.
If causalHow did people come to be in each group? What goes wrong: confounding. Research Methodology takes it up; section 3.5 shows the randomized trial.
TimeInside each: where each person’s clock starts, and occurrence vs ascertainment. Who survived to be seen, when things were measured, what period the number describes. Two dimensions, directionality and timing, later name designs.

Selection bias and information bias are the two classic bias families; here each has a home. The third row has no classic bias name, and confounding belongs to the causal-only question.

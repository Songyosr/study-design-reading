The three practice studies are short and invented. Each is one of chapter 3’s designs, except the trial. Name each design yourself, and take it apart on the worksheet’s five rows before reading the suggested answers.

4.1 · Your turn

Practice the worksheet on a short methods paragraph

In the workshop you will take three real studies apart on one worksheet. Here you practice the same five rows on three invented studies, alone.

First, tag the paragraph. Give each highlighted phrase the tag it answers: goal, time, population, measurement or estimation. Several phrases fit two tags. The feedback says why.

Then fill the five rows.

  • 1 · Goal. Pick one.
  • 2 · Time. Place E (exposure) and O (outcome) on the timeline, around the fixed study start ▼. Choose the way the study traced, and an arrow shows it. Then choose the timing.
  • 3 · Design. Name it, and say why. No phrase names the design. You read it from row 2 and from how people were chosen.
  • 4 · Take it apart. The phrases you tagged become each component’s fact. Then pick the one of chapter 1’s three questions that matters most here. Each question shows its meaning in brackets.
  • 5 · Your own. Here one gap is given. Name its main worry, and say whether they could have done better with what they had. Then write your own sentence, and compare it with one model.

Nothing here is saved. The suggested answers are one reading, not the answer.

What happens in the workshop

You work in groups of three or four. You read three short real studies on your own, then fill one worksheet together, one study per column. Row 5 is yours alone. You can do it on paper or in a shared spreadsheet.

4.2 · Practice

Name what goes wrong

PracticeThe main worry, on nine short studies

The ways a study can go wrong are in section 1.4. Here you practice naming them. This is row 5’s main worry on the worksheet.

Each card describes one invented study and what it reports. Decide what goes wrong, if anything. Some cards have no flaw, one is only chance, and one reads a comparison as a cause. The feedback explains each answer. Several cards have a second defensible reading.

Nothing here is saved. In the workshop you will name the main worry on real papers. Chapter 2’s table shows that each design’s usual worry is one of these failures.

Check yourself. For any choice in a study, ask which alternative explanation it leaves open.
One way to think about it

A clinic sample leaves open that the number reflects who attends clinics. A self-reported diagnosis leaves open that people never diagnosed were missed. A before-and-after rate leaves open that something else changed at the same time. Naming the alternative tells you what evidence to look for next.

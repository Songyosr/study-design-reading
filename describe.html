Descriptive epidemiology: person, place and time

Descriptive epidemiology says how common a health condition is in a defined population, and how that differs by person, place and time. Each split has one question to ask before you believe it. Each section ends where describing turns into explaining.

Outcome 4 · By the end of this chapter you can
  • say what a description is for, and what a good descriptive question states;
  • by person, place and time, choose the right number, check that enough people stand behind it, and check whether the counting changed;
  • spot the ecological fallacy, and write a bounded conclusion with one finding, one limitation and one use.

5.1 · Describe

How common, in whom, where and when

DescribeHow common, in a defined population, by person, place and time. It stops before why

Look at the first two sentences of most papers. One says how common a problem is. The next says who has it most, where, or how fast it is growing. These numbers size up a problem. A health office uses them to decide what comes first, where to put a clinic, and how many places to plan. A researcher uses them to show that a problem matters, and to see where to look next.

Descriptive epidemiology makes these numbers. It says how common a health condition is in a defined population, and how that differs by person, place and time (CDC 2012). Who has it? Where? When? It stops before why.

Figure 5.1 shows the idea. One invented province and one survey give one number, the share of adults with diabetes on a blood test. Split by age, by district and by survey round, it gives three descriptions of the same people. Only the split changes.

Each split comes with one question to ask before you believe it. By person, which number compares fairly? By place, are there enough people behind each number? Over time, did the counting change?

A description is a study too

A good descriptive question states four things (Lesko, Fox & Edwards 2022). The first three are the components from chapter 1. The fourth is what this chapter is about.

  • Population. Whose number is it? Choose the people a decision will affect. In Figure 5.1, they are the province’s adults on the survey date. The sample must stand for them. A case series has patients but no population behind it, so it has no rate.
  • Measurement. What counts as having it? The definition moves the number. In one HIV clinic, 75% of patients had the virus under control on a strict cut-off, and 89% on a looser one (Lesko, Fox & Edwards 2022). Pick the definition that fits the use.
  • Estimation. Which number? A prevalence counts everyone who has it now. It suits planning care. An incidence or a rate counts new cases. It shows how fast they arise.
  • What to split by, and what to even out. Split by the features you want to show, such as age or district. Even out the ones that get in the way of a comparison. Report the plain numbers first. Section 5.2 shows both.

The numerator and the denominator must cover the same people, place and period. Section C.2 compares the sources.

On its own, or with another goal. A description that stands on its own usually covers a large population, such as a nation or a region, and samples it well. Its value is that it stands for people others care about. The share with diabetes at Hogwarts matters mostly to Hogwarts. More often, a description comes inside a study with another goal. It sets the scene in the opening sentences and the first table. Then the paper turns to why. So the goal belongs to each question, not to the whole study.

5.2 · Person

Person: who has it

PersonWho: age, sex, schooling, income, work

The first thing to describe is how much of a problem there is, and in whom. Which groups to split by depends on how the number will be used. Splitting by age shows which ages need care most. Splitting by income or schooling shows who is left behind. A national survey that reports diabetes by age, sex, schooling or income is a description by person.

Read the age bars in Figure 5.1 as a health office would. Diabetes climbs steeply with age. One adult in five aged 65 or over has it, against one in fifty under 35. Each bar is a share of the people in that group, its own numerator over its own denominator. Age is usually the strongest pattern.

These shares come from a survey, so the survey’s own questions apply. Who was examined on the day? Was diabetes found by a blood test, or by asking? Section 3.2 asks them. A survey that picks people unevenly on purpose also needs weights (section C.1). The survey session on 11 November takes weights further.

Sometimes a feature like age matters, but it is not what you want to describe. Say you want to compare two places that differ in age. Then age gets in the way of the comparison, and you need to deal with it.

Before you compare two populations: which number?

Describing still compares, and still uses statistics. “Ten times as common at 65 and over as at 20 to 34” is a description. It becomes a claim about a cause only if it is read as one. A share from a sample comes with an interval, because the sample stands for the population. Standardizing, steadying small places and reading trends are all descriptive tools (Fox et al. 2022; Conroy & Murray 2020).

Hogwarts and PSU, with invented numbers, differ in age. Of Hogwarts’s 400 adults, 240 are 50 or over, against 600 of PSU’s 2,000. The crude share says how common diabetes is among the adults who live in each place. It is higher at Hogwarts. The age-standardized share asks how common it would be if both had the same age mix. Switch the age mix in Figure 5.2.

The shared mix in Figure 5.2 is the two places together. Another standard, such as a national population, would give other numbers, so say which one you used. A count answers yet another question. PSU has more people with diabetes, 160 against 48, so more people who need care.

Specific shares, like the age groups in Figure 5.1, are often the clearest. Crude and standardized shares answer different questions. Standardizing is a choice to justify, not a habit (Lesko, Fox & Edwards 2022; Fox et al. 2022). Done by default, it can hide the differences a description is meant to show (Conroy & Murray 2020).

Why standardize, and not adjust? Many papers “adjust for age” with a regression model. The model gives a coefficient, such as a ratio for place with age held fixed. That is not the share of any stated population. A standardized share still is. It says how common diabetes would be in a stated age mix. So a health office can read it and plan with it. For a description, adjusting is often not needed (Conroy & Murray 2020). It can shrink a real gap between groups, and readers tend to take an adjusted number as a cause (Lesko, Fox & Edwards 2022). Report the plain numbers first.

Check yourself. Hogwarts against PSU: what do the crude and the age-standardized ratios each say?
One way to think about it

The crude ratio, 1.5, says diabetes is more common among the adults who live at Hogwarts. The age-standardized ratio, about 1.1, says that most of that gap comes from Hogwarts’s older population. Both are right. They answer different questions. Asking why the places differ is a causal question. Research Methodology takes it up.

Going further: describing inequality Splitting by one feature at a time can mislead. Diabetes may be more common in older people, and more common in poorer people. That does not say how common it is in older, poorer people. Splitting by two features at once shows this, if each group has enough people (Lesko, Fox & Edwards 2022). Health inequality also has its own summary measures. They sum up a gap across all income or schooling groups, not only the top and the bottom (Mackenbach & Kunst 1997). These methods go beyond this chapter.

5.3 · Place

Place: where it is more common

PlaceWhere: region, province, district, urban or rural

Place works the same way. A rate by province or district shows where the burden is, and where to look first. It is often drawn as a map. Places differ in age too, so maps often show age-standardized rates, as in section 5.2. The count answers a different question. It says where the most people need care.

Before you split by district: are there enough people behind each number?

A survey often records each person’s district. That does not mean it can speak for each district. Most surveys are planned to give one number for the whole province. Each district then has only the people who happened to be sampled there.

Read the map in Figure 5.3. Town and Harbour, on the west coast, have high shares. The Island’s shade is the strongest, but only 60 adults were examined there, 9 of them with diabetes. Four more or four fewer would put its share anywhere between 8% and 22%.

Switch Figure 5.3 to the funnel (Spiegelhalter 2005). Each square is a district. Across is how many adults were examined there. Up is its share. The line across is the province’s share. The curve above it shows how high chance alone could push a district of that size. It is high for small districts and low for large ones. Now draw a few more surveys. The west coast stays above the curve. The Island moves up and down. Pooling several years, smoothing the rates (Elliott & Wartenberg 2004), or giving the count beside each rate also steady small places.

More people in each place: routine records

Routine records are the other common source for maps. They come from care, such as clinic visits, hospital admissions and registries. They cover far more people in each district, so small places are steadier. The price is in who is counted and how. Only people who reach care are counted, and access differs between districts. A district with a hospital finds more of its cases than one far from care. Diagnosis in routine care does not follow one protocol, as a survey’s blood test does. Records also count only diagnosed cases. Check, too, that cases are counted where people live, not where they are treated. Section C.2 compares the sources.

Check yourself. The smallest district had the highest rate this year. Should it get the new clinic?
One way to think about it

Not on this alone. With few residents, chance moves the rate a lot. The smallest districts are the most likely to top or bottom any one year’s list. Look at several years pooled, the interval around each rate, and the number of cases. The number of cases is what the clinic would actually serve.

When rates meet a feature of the place: the ecological study

Rates by place invite a next step. Set them beside a feature of each place, such as the share who drink sweet drinks, or a local tax. One row of data is then a whole group, and the study is ecological (Morgenstern 1995). Ecological says what one row is. Descriptive says what the question is. Textbooks file the ecological study both ways. Some list it among descriptive studies (Grimes & Schulz 2002). Others list it among analytical ones (WHO 2006, Table 3.1).

Read the first tab of Figure 5.4. Districts that drink more sweet drinks have lower glucose on average. It is natural to read this as true of people. Now switch to the people. Taken together, people who drink more have higher glucose. The districts run the other way because they differ in age. A pattern across groups may not hold for the people in them. This is the ecological fallacy (Morgenstern 1995). The study knows each place’s averages, not the people in it.

Setting rates beside a feature of a place still describes. Reading the feature as the reason the rates differ is explaining. That needs a comparison built for it.

Going further: spatial epidemiology Spatial epidemiology does much more with the same data (Elliott & Wartenberg 2004). It asks whether nearby places are more alike than chance allows. It looks for clusters, neighboring places that stay high together. It also uses features of a place, such as the distance to a clinic, beside data on each person. These methods go beyond this chapter. Here, the aim is to read a map with care.

5.4 · Time

Time: is it changing?

TimeWhen: year, season, before and after a date

Time works the same way. A trend shows how a burden moves. Is it growing, and in whom is it growing fastest? It can be split by age or by place too. Across years, the population itself changes. An aging population raises a crude rate with no change in anyone’s risk. So compare years with age-specific or age-standardized rates, as in section 5.2. The count answers a different question. It says how many people will need care.

Figure 5.5 counts something different from the survey in section 5.1. It shows every new diagnosis recorded in the province each year, per 1,000 adults. That is an incidence rate from records, because the question is how many new cases arise each year. Rates also let you compare periods of different length. A count over five years and a count over one year answer different questions. Divide by the time at risk first (section C.4). The rate rises year by year, and a sugar tax starts in 2020.

Before you call it a rise: did the counting change?

A trend in records can move with no change in the disease. Counting can change in three ways. Detection changes with screening or a new test. Definition changes with new criteria. Recording changes with coding, reporting or coverage. If diagnoses rise while deaths stay flat, the likeliest story is more finding, not more disease (Welch, Kramer & Black 2019).

Counting can fall, too. In the UK, new diagnoses of type 2 diabetes fell sharply from April 2020, when COVID-19 interrupted routine care. Each month was compared with the trend of the ten years before. The authors estimate about 60,000 diagnoses missed or delayed between March and December 2020. Diabetes takes years to develop, so the disease itself was unlikely to have fallen (Carr et al. 2021).

When a trend meets a date: the interrupted time series

Now read Figure 5.5 as a question about the tax. “Before against after” compares the average before 2020 with the average from 2020. The rate was already rising, so the later years are higher anyway, and the tax looks harmful. “Carry the old trend forward” asks where the rate would be if the old trend had gone on. The gap between the recorded years and that dashed line is the change after the date. This is the idea behind an interrupted time series (Lopez Bernal, Cummins & Gasparrini 2017).

The gap is the tax’s effect only if nothing else changed at the same time. Check the box for the counting change in Figure 5.5. Screening starts in the same year, and one line can no longer tell the tax from the counting. Other events can land on the same date. In 2020, COVID-19 changed both care and daily life. A place without the tax helps. If its rate moved the same way, the tax is unlikely to be the reason.

Describing the trend is descriptive. Saying the tax changed it is explaining. Workshop 2’s paper is a time study of this kind. It gives rates for all Thai adolescents, period by period, around a policy date. Read it for where it describes and where it explains.

Check yourself. A district’s diabetes rate fell in the two years after a sugar tax. What would you want to know before calling it the tax’s effect?
One way to think about it

Did the fall start before the tax? Look at the yearly series, not one period before and one after. Did the way diabetes was counted change at the same time? Did anything else happen in those years? Did other districts without the tax fall too? Did the people living there change? A before–after comparison of one group describes a change. Explaining it needs a comparison built for it.

Going further: time series Time series is a field of its own. Seasonal patterns can be separated from the trend. Segmented regression puts an interrupted time series into numbers, with an interval. A comparison series from a place without the policy makes it stronger (Lopez Bernal, Cummins & Gasparrini 2017). Age-period-cohort models try to separate age, calendar year and year of birth (Rosenberg & Anderson 2011). Forecasting carries a series forward to plan ahead. These methods go beyond this chapter.

5.5 · Conclusion

A bounded conclusion: one finding, one limitation, one use

ConcludeSay what was found, what could distort it and what it is good for

Workshop 2 ends with a short statement about a real paper. It has one finding, one limitation and one use. This section shows the shape.

A descriptive conclusion has three parts. A finding names the measure, the population and the time. A limitation says what could distort it, and which way it would push the number (Lesko, Fox & Edwards 2022). This chapter’s questions help. Which number? Enough people behind it? Did the counting change? A use says what the finding is good for, such as planning, monitoring, or deciding where to look next.

Two claims need more than a description. Why rates differ or change is a causal question, as at the end of section 5.3 and section 5.4. It needs a comparison built for it. What holds for one person needs more than a share or a place’s average, as the ecological study showed. Overstepping the data is a frequent error in descriptive reports (Grimes & Schulz 2002).

Try the cards. Each is an invented statement. Decide whether it stays within what the data show.

Check yourself. Turn this into a bounded conclusion: “Diabetes is a growing epidemic at PSU.”
One way to think about it

For example: “Among PSU adults, diabetes prevalence measured by fasting glucose rose from 6% to 8% between two surveys (finding). The second survey also changed how people were invited, which could account for part of it (limitation). Clinic places should be planned for the higher number, while the next survey checks the trend (use).” The numbers are invented. The shape is the point.

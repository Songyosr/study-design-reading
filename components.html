C.1 · Population

Population: where selection enters, and how weights undo a planned imbalance

PopulationWho is the answer about, and whose data do we have?

Chapter 1 drew the steps from the target population to the analyzed sample, in Figure 1.1. People fall out at each step. When who falls out depends on what is measured, the answer tilts. This is selection bias. It can enter at four places.

  • The source. A hospital wing is not everyone.
  • The selection. A health fair takes whoever turns up.
  • Refusals. People who decline can differ from people who agree.
  • Missing data. People without a usable reading drop out last.

A random draw from a complete list deals with the first two. It does not settle refusals or missing readings. Some protocols list refusal as an exclusion criterion. But people who decline still belong to the target. So who declines still matters for the answer.

Selection bias is often taught for comparisons between groups. It moves a single prevalence just as well, as Figure C.1 shows.

Other words. Some texts use other names. They call the target population the reference population. They call the source population the study population. And they call our study population the study sample. Hulley and colleagues draw a longer chain. It runs from the target population to the accessible population, then the intended sample, then the actual subjects (Hulley et al. 2013).

Ask, too, what one row of data stands for. It may be a person, a household, an admission or a whole place. One person admitted three times is three admissions. When one row is a place, the study is ecological (section 2.2).

Ask also how people were chosen. A study can choose them by their exposure, by their outcome, or by neither. A sample from a register takes people whatever their diabetes. A case-control study chooses by the outcome, usually because it is rare (section 3.3). How people were chosen is not the same as directionality, though the two usually go together (section 2.1).

Time hereWhen do people qualify? People can become eligible, enter or leave while a study runs. Section 2.1 sets where each person’s clock starts.

One school, three ways to sample it

The simulated Hogwarts in Figure C.1 has 400 adults. Of them, 48 have diabetes. The grid lines people up by blood glucose, highest on the left. So people with diabetes fill the leftmost columns. A real population gives no such clue, and the dots stay faint until the study sees them.

Choose the source and guess the prevalence your sample will show. Then draw. A source that favors people with high glucose, such as the hospital wing, pulls the estimate up. One that favors healthier people, such as a health fair, pulls it down. A tint behind the grid shows which way the source leans, toward people with diabetes or away from them. The caption names each tint.

Then change who agrees to take part. A fair draw can still end with an unfair sample. Real studies never see the dashed line. So the design has to give a reason to trust the sample.

Check yourself. Choose hospital-wing visitors and raise the sample size to 160. Does the gap to the dashed line close? Then choose a random register sample with fewer people with diabetes agreeing. Is a random draw enough?
One way to think about it

No, in both cases. With hospital-wing visitors, the interval narrows, but it stays centered above the true share. A larger sample from the same source repeats the same selection. With a random register sample and fewer people with diabetes agreeing, the invitation was fair, but the people who answered are not. So most draws land below the true share. Precision measures chance. It cannot measure who was more likely to be seen or to say yes.

Weights: the sample’s share or the population’s

A survey can describe a population it did not sample evenly. Large surveys often pick people within age groups on purpose. They take more older adults than their share, so that older groups have enough people for their own estimates.

The raw share with the condition then describes the sample, not the population. A survey weight fixes this. Each person counts for the number of people like them in the population. That number is the inverse of their chance of being picked. The survey chose the imbalance, so it knows exactly how to undo it. Selection that nobody planned, such as refusals, can be repaired only in part, and only under assumptions.

The invented example in Figure C.2 uses PSU, which section 5.2 compares with Hogwarts. PSU has 2,000 adults, 600 of them aged 50 or over. Diabetes affects 5% of the younger and 15% of the older adults, so 8.0% overall.

Weights usually have three layers. The design weight is the inverse of each person’s chance of selection. A second layer corrects for people who did not respond. A third calibrates the weighted totals to census counts by age and sex. The estimate uses the weights. Its confidence interval must also allow for the clusters (villages, enumeration areas) and strata of the sample, or it will be too narrow (Korn & Graubard 1991).

C.2 · Measurement

Measurement: a reference standard, a cut-off and a data source

MeasurementHow were the data obtained, and do they represent what we intend?

Chapter 1 set out validity, reliability and the three places where error enters, in Figure 1.2. This section adds a yardstick, a test to try and the data sources.

The yardstick is the reference standard. It is the best available way to decide who truly has the condition, such as a full work-up for diabetes. Any quicker measure is judged against it. Changing the definition changes the question, not the error.

Source of errorNot valid (wrong the same way each time)Not reliable (different on a repeat)
InstrumentA meter that always reads 10 mg/dL highThe same meter gives slightly different readings on the same blood
CharacteristicOne fasting reading misses diabetes that shows only after mealsGlucose changes with fasting and time of day
ObserverAn interviewer who leads people toward one answerTwo nurses who read the same scale differently

One test, three steps

Figure C.3 follows 400 simulated adults through three steps. Filled dots truly have diabetes by the reference standard. Open dots do not. Each dot sits at its usual reading on a single fasting test. The dots never move. Only the test’s verdict changes.

1 · Ideal. A line that follows the truth separates every filled dot from every open one. Only someone who already knows who has diabetes could draw it.

2 · One cut-off. A real test can only draw a straight line on the reading. Where the two piles overlap, open dots above the line are false positives. Filled dots below it are false negatives. The key shows how each is marked. Move the line and you trade one for the other. The readout gives the test’s sensitivity and specificity. The diagnostic-test session takes these further.

3 · Measure again. A repeat reading of the same person lands a little differently. Time of day, the meter and the observer all play a part. People inside the shaded band can land on either side, so the count changes from repeat to repeat. Notice which way it drifts on average. An error that is random in each reading can still push the count one way. Studying more people does not remove it.

What does shrink the drift is more readings per person, not more people. Averaging two or three readings brings the count back near the 55 that usual readings give. It never reaches the true 48, because a fixed error in the test survives any number of repeats. Only a better test removes it. Each extra reading costs another visit, and fewer people may come back. A fix in one component can cost something in another.

Check yourself. In step 2, move the cut-off to 110, then to 140. Which kind of error grows each time, and which way does the count move? Then, in step 3, press Measure again a few times.
One way to think about it

At 126 the test counts 55 against the 48 who truly have diabetes. That is 13 false positives and 6 false negatives, so the count is pushed up. At 110 it finds everyone with diabetes, but false positives rise to 75 and the count to 123. At 140 false positives almost vanish (2), but false negatives rise to 25 and the count falls to 25. No line is right in general. Screening may favor the low line, and confirming a diagnosis the high one. In step 3 the count changes with every repeat, mostly a little above 55, and averages about 57. The error is random in each reading, yet it pulls the count one way. Studying more people would not remove it. Averaging two or three readings per person would shrink it, though never to 48, because the test’s fixed error remains.

When groups are compared. Error that is the same in both groups usually blurs a real difference. Error that differs between them can create one. So measure each thing the same way in every group. Keep the person measuring unaware of the group where you can. In self-report the person is the observer, so memory and willingness to answer become part of the measurement.

Other words. Validity is also called accuracy, and reliability repeatability. Some texts call reliability precision. This reading keeps “precision” for chance. Measurement texts often name the three sources a phenomenon, an instrument and an observer. Characteristic is our word for phenomenon.

Time hereWhen did the event happen, when was it measured, and when did the team collect it? What period does a report cover? Section 2.1 and section C.4 keep these apart.

What each data source can count

Measurement starts before the test. The data source decides what can be counted at all. So it decides which number a description can give (section 5.1). Choose a source in Figure C.4 and read what it counts, what it divides by and what it misses.

A survey that tests everyone gives a prevalence on the survey date. It includes cases nobody had diagnosed. A disease registry counts new diagnoses, so it gives an incidence. But it counts only cases that reached a doctor and were reported. Hospital admissions count events, not people. One person admitted twice counts twice. Clinic records count the people who come. Death registration counts deaths, by the cause written on the certificate.

C.3 · Estimation

Estimation: one group of people, five numbers

EstimationWhat number, exactly, and compared with what?

Chapter 1 took four numbers from twelve staff, in Figure 1.3. Figure C.5 does the same with twenty people over one year, and lets you switch between them. It adds two lessons. The day you look changes a prevalence. And people who leave turn a risk into a range.

Figure C.5 draws each person as a line, in the marks of chapter 3. The thick part is what the chosen number observes. A filled dot is a case it counts. An open dot records no diabetes. An arrow means follow-up stopped while the person could still get diabetes.

  • A count is the filled dots. It has no denominator.
  • A prevalence looks at one day. Its denominator is everyone there that day.
  • A risk follows everyone free of diabetes on 1 January. Its denominator is those people.
  • A rate adds up the thick lines. Its denominator is the person-time.

Now switch on departures. Two people move away free of diabetes. Nobody knows what happened to them after they left. So the risk becomes a range. The rate keeps the months they were followed. It still assumes that leaving had nothing to do with getting diabetes.

Person 15 dies free of diabetes. Some texts treat that death like a move. The two choices answer different questions. Counting the death as a non-case gives the risk in the real world, where people die of other things. Treating it like a move asks what the risk would have been if no one had died. Neither is wrong, so say which one you mean.

Other names. The rate is also called incidence density. Attack rate and cumulative incidence are risks, so always say over what period.

Comparison: like with like

A comparison is optional, and it can still be descriptive. Use the same number in both groups, over the same period. Then ask how people ended up in each group. It may be by who they are, by their own exposure, by their outcome, by date, or by an assigned intervention. Two groups give a difference or a ratio of risks, rates, prevalences or odds. When people were chosen by their outcome, the odds ratio is the comparison available (section 3.3).

When the groups are formed by an exposure, such as daily sweet drinks, the comparison describes an association. On its own, it does not say why the groups differ. Depending on the goal, it can be read as a pattern, a predictor or evidence of a cause.

C.4 · Time

Time: two dimensions on one clock

TimeWhen it happened, when it was measured, when it was collected

Section 2.1 split time into two dimensions, directionality and timing. Each has its own figure there, Figure 2.1 and Figure 2.2. Figure C.6 draws both on one clock, in the same marks. So you can move one and watch the other stay still.

Slide the study start

Choose which way the study traced, and the events its data refer to. Then slide the study start ▼. As the start moves later, a window such as 2022–2028 turns from concurrent to mixed to historical. The arrows stay as they are. Switch to backward, and only the arrows turn.

Each person has their own time zero and end. The window is a calendar window, not anyone’s follow-up. So a database covering 2019 to 2023 has not followed everyone for five years.

One date has no direction. Exposure and outcome are taken together, as in a survey. Its timing can still be historical or concurrent, as the grid in section 2.2 shows. It is never mixed.

Time in each component

Population

Who is still there when you look?

A survey counts the people there on its day. People who died or moved away are missing. People who live long with the disease are over-represented. Section 3.2 shows it with row 1 of the twenty staff.

Measurement

Measured before or after the outcome is known?

An exposure can happen long before the outcome and still be measured after it. Then the outcome can color the answer. Section 3.3 shows the look-back.

Estimation

Over what period?

A one-year risk and a five-year risk differ for the same people. A prevalence depends on how many new cases arise, and on how long people live with the condition. Periods of different length need rates, as the box below shows.

A trap: periods of different length

Hogwarts’s hospital wing saw 600 visits for broom crashes in the five years before new flying rules. It saw 250 in the one year after. Did visits fall or rise? Switch between the two ways of counting.

Going further: lead time, overdiagnosis and the limits of the deaths check

Lead time gives a temporary jump in diagnoses. Overdiagnosis keeps them raised. Survival from diagnosis then looks better, even if nobody lives longer. Deaths are a useful check, but not a perfect one. Better treatment lowers them. Changes in how causes of death are certified and coded move them too (Welch, Kramer & Black 2019; WHO 2006, chapter 2).

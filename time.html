Time, and the design names it gives

"Time takes it all, whether you want it to or not" - Stephen King

Two questions about time sort most studies into their familiar names: which way did the study trace, and when did its events happen?

By the end of this chapter you can
  • place exposure and outcome on a timeline, and say which way the study traced;
  • say whether a study is historical, mixed or concurrent, and what that buys;
  • name a design from its features, say whether it studies people or groups, and say when each design is the usual choice.

2.1 · Directionality and timing

Two time dimensions: directionality and timing

TimeWhich way did the study trace? Did E and O happen before the study started?

Each variable has three moments: when it happened, when it was measured (an answer given, a chart entry written, a test done), and when the team collected it. It could be the same date, or different. Keeping them apart gives two dimensions of time: Directionality and Timing. Together with the other components, they name the designs (section 2.2).

Directionality: which way did the study trace?

In reality, people move through time. They become at risk, may be exposed, and may develop the outcome. A study cannot watch all of this, so it takes a route in. The usual way to draw the route is an arrow between exposure (E) and outcome (O) (Kramer & Boivin 1987), as in Figure 2.1:

  • Forward, E → O: start with people at risk at time zero, with their exposure, then see who develops the outcome.
  • Backward, E ← O: start with the outcome, then look back for exposure. The controls stand for the population at risk behind the cases (Vandenbroucke & Pearce 2012).
  • Non-directional, E and O together: take one slice, and see both at the same date.

Each person’s clock starts at the tail of the arrow. Going forward, it starts at time zero. Going back, it starts at the index date: the case’s outcome date, which its controls are given too. For a slice, it is the survey date, the study’s time anchor (Hernán et al. 2016; Dickerman et al. 2020). Groups that are compared must start their clocks the same way.

Directionality is not how people were chosen, though the two usually go together. Most backward studies choose people by their outcome. But a study can also start with a whole register, sort everyone by outcome, and trace back (Kramer & Boivin 1987).

Timing: did E and O happen before the study started collecting?

Timing compares when E and O happened with the study start ▼, the date the team starts collecting. The team always collects after the start. What differs is whether the events, and the records of them, came before it. Place E and O where they happened for a typical person:

  • Historical: both before the start.
  • Mixed: one on each side, usually the exposure before and the outcome after.
  • Concurrent: both after the start (Kramer & Boivin 1987).

Timing matters because data the team plans are usually measured better. When the team collects data as events happen, it can decide who enters and leaves, how each measurement is defined, which tools measure it and how often. Historical data come with the records’ own definitions, coverage and coding changes. And if an important variable was never measured, nothing can add it later.

Check yourself. A team collects data in one afternoon from hospital files covering 2019 to 2023. Is the study cross-sectional?
One way to think about it

Not necessarily. If the files follow each person over time, the study is longitudinal and done from records, even though the data were gathered in one afternoon. Cross-sectional means each person’s state is summarised at one time anchor. The afternoon is when the team read the files, not when E and O happened. In the two dimensions: the study traces forward from each person’s time zero, through records made before the study began, so the directionality is forward. Both E and O happened before ▼, so the timing is historical.

2.2 · Name it

Read a design off a study

Name itTraced which way? Happened when? How did the groups form? What is one row?

Directionality names three designs:

  • Forward, from the exposure or from everyone, to the outcome: a cohort.
  • Backward, from the outcome, with cases and controls: a case-control study.
  • Non-directional, exposure and outcome at one date: cross-sectional.

Timing adds a second word. For a cohort, prospective means concurrent, and retrospective means historical. For other designs the words are used loosely, so say which you mean (Vandenbroucke et al. 2007, STROBE). Most case-control studies, for example, are mixed (Kramer & Boivin 1987).

How the groups formed names one more. If the investigator assigned the exposure by chance, the study is a randomised trial; by another rule, a non-randomised trial.

The direction usually sets when exposure is measured. In a snapshot, exposure is measured at the same time as the outcome. In a cohort, it is measured before the outcome. In a case-control study, it is usually measured after the outcome is known, and that is where recall comes in. A nested case-control study is the exception: it can use exposure recorded long before the outcome.

One row: a person, or a group?

One more question cuts across every design: what is one row of data? Usually it is a person. When it is a whole group, a place or a period, the study is the ecological version of its design (Morgenstern 1995). For example:

  • rates across provinces on one date;
  • one country’s rates, period by period, around a policy date;
  • provinces followed forward from a reform, those that took it against those that did not: a cohort of provinces.

The answer is then about groups, and may not hold for the people in them. Chapter 5 takes this up under place (section 5.3).

Reading the grid. Down a column, the timing is the same and the direction differs. Along a row, one design takes each timing word. Every cell is possible except one, so backward does not mean old data, and a study can follow people forward through old records. Every cell also has a group version, when one row is a place or a period. A randomised trial sits in the forward row, almost always concurrent, with its groups formed by chance. Going further: many more names

Many more names are built from the same parts. A repeated cross-sectional survey takes new people each round. A nested case-control study draws its cases and controls inside a cohort, and a case-cohort study is a close relative. In a case-crossover study, each case is its own control at an earlier time. An ambidirectional cohort has mixed timing. Before–after studies, interrupted time series, and cluster and non-randomised trials are built the same way. The EPI-II lectures on each design take them further.

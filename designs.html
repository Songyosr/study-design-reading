3.1 · At a glance

At a glance: what each design adds

OverviewStudy design in a nutshell

Chapters 1 and 2 gave you the parts: population, measurement and estimation, and time. This chapter puts them to work. We run four diabetes studies, a survey, a case-control study, a cohort, and a trial, in one invented scenario: the twenty staff of Hogwarts, from 2016 to 2026.

In this setting, each person joined the staff at some point, and some later moved away or died. Some drink sweet drinks daily, and some get diabetes. Figure 3.1 draws each person as a line. Every design looks at the same twenty lives. What changes is which part of each life it uses, and so what it can see.

To read the lines:

  • Each line is one person, from joining to 2026.
  • Its color shows whether they drink daily (see the key).
  • Thick means the design observes them. Thin means it does not.
  • The line is dashed once they have diabetes.
  • Filled means diabetes. Open means none. A dot is where the design records it.
  • An arrow means follow-up stopped while they could still get diabetes.

Each section takes one design, component by component. The first two designs see each person once. The last two follow people from time zero. Each section starts with when that design is usually chosen. That depends on the question, and on limits such as how rare the outcome is, time and money, ethics, or the data that exist. It is the usual situation, not a rule.

In a nutshell, the table below gives the main concern of each design, in each component. Do not worry if a cell is new. Each section explains its row.

3.2 · Survey

A cross-sectional survey: who is there on the day

SurveyUsually chosen when the question is how common something is now

A survey is the usual choice when the question is how common something is now. Each person is seen once, in one short period. Since there is no follow-up to pay for, a survey can reach many more people, with a sample that stands for a whole region or country. It can also measure each person well, with an examination and blood tests. Many countries run a national health examination survey this way. The price is time order. Exposure and outcome are measured together.

In Figure 3.2, the survey looks at the same twenty staff, on one day.

Population

Who gets in? Many people, but only those there on the day

A survey can take a large random sample, so it can stand for a whole population. But it sees only who is there on the day. In 2026 that is 16 of the 20 staff. In Figure 3.2, look at row 1. This drinker got diabetes in 2017 and died in 2019. The 2026 survey never sees them. So 3 of 6 drinkers have diabetes (50%). Count row 1 too, and it would be 4 of 7 (57%). People who die soon after the illness starts drop out. People who live long with it stay. This is survivorship bias. It does not matter for “how common is it today?” It matters for “what causes it?”

Measurement

When is each thing measured? Both at one visit

Drinking and diabetes are measured at the same visit. So the survey cannot say which came first. Someone told they have diabetes may already drink less. This is the main price of a survey. It goes away when the exposure is fixed before the outcome could start, such as blood group. One visit also leaves money for good measurement: an examination and a blood test, not just questions.

Estimation

Which number? A prevalence, not a risk

A survey sees each person only once. So it cannot tell new cases from old ones. It gives a prevalence: the share with diabetes on that day, and from it comes a prevalence ratio, or a prevalence odds ratio. It gives no risk and no incidence rate. On the 2026 survey day, 3 of 6 daily drinkers (50%) and 3 of 10 others (30%) have diabetes. The prevalence ratio is 1.7. The cohort tab in Figure 3.1 follows the same lives and finds a risk ratio of 2.8. The survey misses row 1, who died. It also counts row 9, who had diabetes when hired.

Check yourself. A school nurse tests every first-year who comes to the screening day. Those absent are not seen again. A limitation they could not avoid, or a choice they could have made better?
One way to think about it

It depends on what a second visit would have cost. If one more morning would have reached most of the absent pupils, skipping it was a choice they could have made better. Absent pupils may differ: more often ill, or new to the school. If the nurse had one day for the whole school, it is a limitation they could not avoid. Then report how many were absent, and which way that might push the share. Chapter 4’s first practice study is this one: try the worksheet on it.

3.3 · Case-control

A case-control study: where the controls come from

Case-controlUsually chosen when the outcome is rare

A case-control study is the usual choice when the outcome is rare or slow to appear. Not always, though. Outbreak teams use one even when many people fell ill, because it is fast. It starts from the outcome. It takes the new cases in a population over a period, as they happen or from past records. It samples controls, people without the outcome, from the same population. Then it looks back at the exposure of both. It saves time and money, because it needs all the cases but only a few of the others. The price is choosing the controls well, and measuring exposure after the outcome.

Population

Who gets in? Cases, and controls from the same population

The cases are the six staff who got diabetes between 2016 and 2026. The controls must come from the population that produced them (Vandenbroucke & Pearce 2012). To check, take any control. Suppose they got diabetes. Would this study count them as a case? For the staff, yes, because the staff clinic would find it. In Figure 3.3, each case's shaded band holds the staff still free that day. Its control is drawn from there. Now switch Figure 3.3 to a health fair. These six people are not staff. If they got diabetes, the staff clinic would never see it. So their drinking need not match the staff's. People at a health fair may care more about their health, and fewer may drink sweet drinks daily.

Measurement

When is each thing measured? The outcome first, then a look back

Drinking before diagnosis is asked about, or read from records. It is the thick stretch before each dot in Figure 3.3. Cases may search their memory harder than controls (recall), and an interviewer who knows who is a case may ask differently. Exposure written in records, or samples stored, before the outcome avoid both.

Estimation

Which number? An odds ratio

Among the six cases, 4 drank daily and 2 did not. Among the six staff controls, 2 did and 4 did not. The odds ratio is (4/2) ÷ (2/4) = 4.0. That is close to the rate ratio for the whole staff: 3.8, in Figure 3.5 (see “what a case-control odds ratio estimates” below). With health-fair controls, only 1 of 6 drinks daily. The odds ratio jumps to 10.0. There is no risk on its own, because the team chose how many controls to take.

How much does it save? Suppose a new glucose-lowering potion is suspected of causing a liver injury in about one in 10,000 users a year. A cohort would need about a million person-years to see 100 cases. A case-control study takes the 100 cases and four controls each: 500 people. Near no effect, it keeps roughly four fifths of the precision (Ury 1975). Set the sliders to one case in every 10,000 and four controls per case to see it, then move them.

Going further: what a case-control odds ratio estimates

It depends on how the controls were sampled. Suppose controls are drawn from people still at risk when each case occurs (density sampling, the shaded bands in Figure 3.3). Then the odds ratio estimates the rate ratio, with no rare-disease assumption. If they are a random sample of everyone at the start (a case-cohort design), it estimates the risk ratio. If you know what fraction of the source population the controls represent, absolute risks can be recovered too. So “no risk on its own” is the safe short version (Vandenbroucke & Pearce 2012).

Going further: when the k/(k+1) rule holds

Ury (1975) derived it for matched designs, for tests near no effect. When the exposure is rare among controls, extra controls per case help more than the rule suggests. Beyond four or five controls per case the gain is usually small.

Check yourself. A team picks its controls from patients with broken bones from broom crashes. When is that reasonable, and when could they have done better?
One way to think about it

Reasonable if no register exists, the broken-bone patients come from the same population as the cases, and their potion use is unrelated to their injury. If the potion makes people dizzy, and dizziness causes crashes, the controls take the potion more often than their population does. Then the odds ratio is pulled toward 1. With a register available, controls drawn by chance from it would have been better.

3.4 · Cohort

A cohort: who gets in, and who leaves

CohortUsually chosen when you need new cases and the order of events

A cohort is the usual choice when you need new cases and the order of events. It can also study several outcomes at once. Everyone starts free of the outcome, at time zero. Here that is the day they joined the staff. Then each person is followed forward. Figure 3.5 first draws the lives in calendar years. Switch its clock to time since joining, and everyone lines up at time zero. Each line's length is then that person's time at risk. This is the view a cohort analyzes. The price is follow-up. It takes time, some people leave, and the outcome must be common enough to collect cases. A very large cohort, such as one built on a national register, can study even a rare outcome.

A prospective cohort starts now and follows people as events happen. In chapter 2's words, its timing is concurrent. A retrospective cohort starts later and reads records made in the past. Its timing is historical. Both trace forward from time zero. So why run a prospective cohort, if the records already exist? Because the team decides who is followed, and how and how often each thing is measured. Records are cheaper and faster, but they come as they are. Each card below shows what that means.

Population

Who gets in, and who stays? Everyone free at the start; then some leave

Everyone free of diabetes when they joined enters: 19 of the 20 staff. Row 9 already had it, so they are not in. Then rows 6 and 18 move away. In Figure 3.5 their lines end in an arrow: no diabetes yet, and nobody knows what came after. This is loss to follow-up. If those who leave differ from those who stay, in ways linked to the outcome, the answer tilts. A prospective team can keep in touch and trace people who move. A retrospective team sees only what the records kept.

Measurement

When is each thing measured? Exposure first, then the outcome

Exposure is measured before the outcome starts, so the outcome cannot color it. That is the cohort's strength. How the outcome is found depends on the style. A prospective cohort sets its own test, such as a glucose test every year. A retrospective cohort reads clinic records made for other reasons. There a case shows up late, at the next visit, or never. Switch Figure 3.5 to clinic records. Rows 1, 2, 3, 10 and 11 are found late, so more of their lives count as free. Row 4's diabetes starts after their last visit, so the records never show it. The drinkers' rate falls from 10.8 to 6.5 per 100 person-years, and the rate ratio from 3.8 to 2.3. Records made for other reasons may hold drinking poorly too. Then some people sit in the wrong group, and the difference usually shrinks. Judge the actual records, though. A well-kept register can beat a rushed protocol.

Estimation

Which number? A risk, or a rate

By 2026, 4 of 8 daily drinkers (50%) and 2 of 11 others (18%) get diabetes. That is a risk ratio of 2.8. But the 2 who moved could have got diabetes later. So each risk is really a range: 50% to 63%, and 18% to 27%. A rate copes better with people who leave part-way. It counts new cases per person-year: each person's time at risk, from time zero until a case is found or they leave. In Figure 3.5, drinkers have 4 cases in 37.0 person-years: 10.8 per 100. Others have 2 cases in 70.5: 2.8 per 100. The rate ratio is 3.8. The components page shows both.

Going further: when entry comes after time zero

Suppose a registry enrolls people at their first clinic visit, months after diagnosis, and reports survival from diagnosis. Anyone who died before reaching the clinic never enters, so survival looks better than it is. The clock starts at diagnosis, but people can only be seen from their first visit. Time zero and entry do not line up.

Check yourself. A tenth of a cohort moved away and could not be followed. A limitation they could not avoid, or a choice they could have made better?
One way to think about it

It depends on what tracing would have cost. If the school kept no forwarding addresses, it is a limitation. Report how many were lost, and give the risk as a range. If the school office held every new address, and a letter would have reached most of them, not tracing them was a choice they could have made better. 3.5 · Trial

A randomized trial: chance decides the exposure

TrialUsually chosen when you can assign the exposure, ethically and affordably

A trial is a cohort in which the team decides the exposure. Why decide it? In the cohort, people chose whether to drink daily. Daily drinkers may also differ in other ways, such as diet or exercise. Some of these factors are linked to the exposure and also cause the outcome. They mix their own effect into the comparison. This is called confounding. Suppose daily drinkers eat worse, and a poor diet causes diabetes. Then part of the drinkers’ extra risk comes from diet, not from the drinks.

While a cohort can adjust for the differences it measured, it cannot account for those it missed. In a trial, chance decides the arm. So every other difference, measured or not, is shared out by chance. On average, the arms start alike.

The price is what can be assigned. The team cannot ask people to drink sweet drinks daily. So here it tests a program to cut them instead. Trials also cost more, take only volunteers, and test one or two planned questions. A cohort can look at many.

Population

Whom do they stand for? Only the volunteers

Only people who meet the entry rules and agree take part. Here that is 14 of the 20. Row 9 already had diabetes, and 5 others said no. Their rows in Figure 3.6 stay thin. They may not resemble the people the answer is for (Westreich et al. 2019). That matters where the program works differently in people unlike them (Rothman, Gallacher & Hatch 2013).

Measurement

Who knows the arm? Not the person measuring

Someone who knows which arm a person is in may record the outcome differently. For example, a nurse who knows a person is in the program may read a borderline glucose as normal. Keep the measurer unaware of the arm where you can. This is called masking, or blinding. Or use a measurement that leaves little to judgment, such as a laboratory glucose test.

Estimation

Compared with whom? Arms decided by randomization

Chance makes the arms alike at the start, on average (Hernán & Robins 2016). In Figure 3.6, randomization put 3 daily drinkers in each arm. The program works here. In it, rows 3 and 4 stay free of diabetes until 2026. So 1 of 7 in the program and 2 of 7 as usual get diabetes, a risk ratio of 0.50. Chance also leaves gaps, and in 14 people they can be large. So a trial asks whether a difference is bigger than chance. Switch to people’s own choice. Most daily drinkers stay as usual, and the program looks far stronger, at 0.25. And the effect of being assigned is not the effect of taking the program.

Check yourself. A trial’s nurses knew each patient’s arm and judged the outcome by eye. A limitation they could not avoid, or a choice they could have made better?
One way to think about it

If a blood test, or an assessor who did not know the arm, was available, it was a choice they could have made better. If neither existed, say so, and say which way it could push the result. The trials session on 2 December takes this further.

i · Start here

You already know the design names. This reading is about why they exist

In journal club, in your proposals and perhaps in your work, you have met names such as cross-sectional survey, case-control study, cohort study and randomized controlled trial. Textbooks often sort them with a tree of questions, like the one in Figure 0.1.

Each of those designs exists for a reason. And each pays somewhere for what it buys: in who was counted, how things were measured, or what number came out. This brief reading gives you the tools to see where.

Chapter 1 recaps the three questions to ask of any study: population, measurement and estimation. Chapter 2 adds time, which turns out to name most designs. Chapter 3 takes each design in turn and asks only what is specific to it. Chapter 4 is practice for Workshop 1. Chapter 5 is descriptive epidemiology, by person, place and time.

One question runs through it all: could they have done better with what they had? A gap the team could not avoid is a limitation to state. A gap it could have closed is a choice it could have made better.

ii · A number to question

A headline number is not an answer until you can say who, how and what

QuestionWhat do we want to learn?

Read the headline in Figure 0.2. Before believing it, what would you want to know? Tap the questions you would ask, and see which component each one probes.

Most questions people ask about a number fall into three groups: who was counted, how the condition was decided, and what the number actually is. Time runs under all three: when people were counted, when the condition was measured, and what date or period the number describes.

Check yourself. Which of your questions would a bigger sample never answer?
One way to think about it

A bigger sample narrows the interval around the estimate. It cannot tell you who was never invited, how diabetes was decided, what the number divides by or when it was counted. Those are design questions, not sample-size questions.

iii · Goals and the move

The goal sets the ideal; the components show the gap

Every study serves a goal and is built from components. Epidemiologic questions serve three broad goals: description, prediction and causal explanation (Shmueli 2010; Hernán, Hsu & Healy 2019). Each goal sets an ideal study, the one that would answer the question directly. For description: everyone in the target population, measured perfectly, at the stated time. For prediction: the people the prediction will be used on, with only what is known at that moment. For causal explanation: a trial in which the groups differ only in the exposure (Hernán & Robins 2016).

Clue words in a study’s aim help. “How common” or “how many” points to description. “Who will” or “predict” points to prediction. “Effect of”, “increases the risk” or “worked” points to a cause. “Associated with” or “linked to” alone leaves the goal unclear: say so, and say why. Taking a study apart means comparing what it did with that ideal. The goals get proper time in the Research Methodology session on conceptual frameworks.

Every chapter uses the components: the population, the measurement and the estimation, with time running through all three. Together they define the estimand: the exact quantity a study is after (ICH E9(R1) 2019). The split lines up with a published framework for well-defined questions (Lesko, Fox & Edwards 2022). It asks for a target population, an outcome and a measure of occurrence, anchored in time. The wording of the three questions on this page is ours. When the goal is causal, a fourth question applies: how did people come to be in each group? Research Methodology takes it up. Section 3.5 shows the one design that answers it by construction: the randomized trial.

The move, in four steps
  1. Write the target first: whom, what, when and which number. That sentence is the estimand.
  2. Picture the ideal study that the goal calls for.
  3. Compare what the study did with that ideal, component by component, and name each gap: could they have done better with what they had?
  4. Name the design last, as a check on your reading. Design names are shorthand for these choices; chapter 2 maps them.

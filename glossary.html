Glossary

Words used on this page, in plain terms

Goal
Description
A goal: how much, where, when and in whom. It characterises the world as it is, and it may compare groups.
Prediction
A goal: who is likely to develop an outcome. Predictors need not be causes. Covered properly in Research Methodology.
Causal explanation
A goal: what would change under an action. It always compares with what would have happened otherwise.
Ideal study
The study that would answer the question directly. For description, it is everyone in the target population, measured perfectly at the stated time. For prediction, it is the people the prediction will be used on, with what is known then. For causal explanation, it is a trial in which groups differ only in the exposure.
Confounding
Only for causal goals. The groups compared differ in more than the exposure, because of how people came to be in each group. Taken up in Research Methodology.
Population
Target population
The people the answer is about, defined by person, place and time. Also called the reference population.
Source population
Where the study draws its people from, such as a registry, a clinic or a fair. In a case-control study, the population that produces the cases. Some texts call it the study population.
Study population
The people who are selected and take part.
Analysed sample
The study population minus anyone whose data are incomplete: the last drop-out.
Selection bias
Distortion that arises when who ends up in the study depends on what is being measured. It can happen at any step from target to analysed sample.
Participation
Who agrees to take part once invited. People who decline can differ from those who agree.
Unit of observation
What one row of data represents: a person, a household, an admission or a whole population.
Measurement
Definition
The rule that decides what counts, and so who truly has the condition.
Reference standard
The best available way to decide who truly has the condition. A quicker measure is judged against it. Also called the gold standard.
Validity
Whether a measure captures what is intended. For a test: whether people with the condition test positive, and people without it test negative. Also called accuracy.
Reliability
Whether a measure gives the same answer when repeated on the same person, by the same or another observer. Also called repeatability. (This reading keeps “precision” for chance.)
Observer
Whoever records the value: a technician, an interviewer, or the person themselves in self-report.
Instrument
The tool or question used: a meter, a questionnaire, a record form.
Characteristic
What is measured, such as blood glucose, depressive status or household wealth. Also called the phenomenon.
Measurement error
The difference between a recorded value and the true value. Systematic error harms validity. Random error harms reliability, and near a cut-off it can still bias a count (section C.2).
Information bias
Measurement error that distorts an estimate. Also called measurement bias.
Differential measurement error
Error that differs between the groups being compared, for example when cases recall exposure more carefully than controls. It can create or inflate an association. Error that is the same in both groups usually blurs one.
False positive
Tests positive although the reference standard says the person does not have the condition. It pushes a count up.
False negative
Tests negative although the person has the condition. It pushes a count down.
Sensitivity
The share of people with the condition whom the test finds.
Specificity
The share of people without the condition whom the test clears.
Proxy
An observable stand-in for something harder to measure, such as a laboratory test standing in for a clinic visit.
Estimation
Estimand
The exact quantity a study is after: the population, what is measured and the summary, anchored in time. Keep it separate from the method that estimates it (the estimator) and the number obtained (the estimate).
A different question answered
When the number computed is not the quantity meant. For example: a crude rate read as a standardised one, or deaths treated as missing when the question was a real-world risk. This is our own label. It is close to a mismatch between the estimand and what was estimated.
Count
The number of people or events. It describes burden. On its own, it cannot compare risk between populations of different size.
Prevalence
The share of a population who have a condition at a stated time.
Risk
The share of people free of a condition at the start who develop it over a stated period. Also called cumulative incidence.
Rate
New cases divided by the total person-time at risk, for example per 100 person-years. Also called incidence rate, or incidence density.
Person-time
The total time people were observed while at risk of becoming a case.
Mean
The average of a measured value, such as glucose. It needs no case definition.
Loss to follow-up
Leaving observation before the end, so what happens afterwards is unknown. Analyses often treat these people as censored.
Competing event
An event, such as death, after which the outcome of interest can no longer occur. Some texts count it as a withdrawal. Here, it is not missing information.
Crude and age-standardised
A crude prevalence describes the people who actually live there. An age-standardised one asks how common the condition would be with a shared age make-up. Each answers its own question.
Chance
Sampling variation: a different random sample gives a slightly different number. A bigger sample shrinks it, but it fixes no bias.
Association
A comparison in which the groups are formed by an exposure, read as a relationship between the exposure and the outcome. It may be causal, predictive or neither.
Comparison
A contrast between groups or periods. How the groups were formed, by place, date, outcome or an assigned intervention, shapes what it can show.
Difference and ratio
Two ways to express a comparison: subtract one value from the other, or divide one by the other.
Designs (chapters 2 and 3)
Odds and odds ratio
Recalled from term 1. Odds are a probability divided by one minus that probability. An odds ratio divides one group’s odds by another’s. When people are chosen by their outcome, the exposure odds ratio is the comparison available (section 3.3).
Cross-sectional study
Everyone measured once, at one time anchor (a date or a short survey period). Exposure and outcome are measured together. Gives a prevalence.
Cohort study
Traced forward. People free of the outcome, chosen by exposure or simply as everyone, are followed to it. Gives risks and rates.
Case-control study
Traced back. Cases, and controls from the population that produced them, are compared for earlier exposure. Usually mixed timing. Gives an odds ratio.
Randomised trial
The investigator assigns the exposure by chance, then follows people forward.
Ecological study
One row of data is a group, such as a place or a period, not a person.
Structure and goal
A design name describes structure: direction, timing, how groups formed, and the unit. “Descriptive” describes a goal, and a goal can use any structure.
Prospective and retrospective cohort
Concurrent and historical timing. For other designs the words are used loosely, so say which dimension you mean.
Efficiency
Precision for the effort spent. With k controls per case, a case-control study keeps about k/(k+1) of the precision every non-case would give.
Ecological fallacy
Reading a pattern across groups as a pattern within persons.
Before–after study and interrupted time series
A before–after study compares one population in two periods. An interrupted time series uses a series of periods with a break at a date. The trend before the date is projected forward.
Describing (chapter 5)
Descriptive study
A study that answers what, who, where and when for a defined population: the case definition, then person, place and time. It stops before why.
Descriptive question
Who and where, anchored in time. What outcome, and which measure. Which characteristics to report separately or standardise over.
Person, place and time
The three ways to split a description: for whom, where and when.
Case series
A description of patients with a condition, often the first sign of something new. No population stands behind it, so it gives no rate.
Specific rate
A rate within one group, such as one age group. Often clearer than a single standardised number.
Numerator and denominator
The events counted and the people (or person-time) they come from. Both must cover the same people, place and period.
Artefact
A change in a recorded rate that comes from how it was counted, not from a change in health. It can come through detection, definition, recording or the denominator.
Small-number instability
Large swings in rates for places with few people, from chance alone.
Funnel plot
A chart of each place's rate against its size, with a limit that shows how high chance alone could push a place of that size. The limit is wide for small places and narrow for large ones.
Cluster
Neighbouring places that stay high together, more than chance alone would give.
Survey weight
How many people in the population each sampled person stands for: the inverse of their chance of being picked.
Weight, stratify, standardise, adjust
Weight: make the sample stand for the population. Stratify: report specific rates. Standardise: recalculate on a chosen common make-up. Adjust, in the causal sense, removes confounding. It is not a descriptive move.
Lead time and overdiagnosis
Screening finds cases earlier (lead time). It also finds some that would never have caused harm (overdiagnosis). Both raise recorded diagnoses.
Bounded conclusion
A finding, a limitation and a use, without claiming why or claiming any one person’s risk.
Age, period and cohort
Three time patterns that can look alike: ageing, the calendar period, and the generation born together.
Time, in every component
Time anchor
The date or period at which a snapshot describes each person.
Time zero (time origin)
The moment each person’s follow-up starts in a study that runs forward, such as enrolment or diagnosis. People should qualify at that same moment, and for causal questions be assigned then too. This is time-zero alignment.
Index date
In a study that enrols people on their outcome, the date of each case’s outcome. Controls are given the same date, and exposure is counted back from it.
Occurrence and ascertainment
When something happened, when it was measured, and when the team collected it.
Directionality
Which way a study traced. Forward: from exposure to outcome, each clock starting at time zero. Backward: from the outcome back to exposure, from the index date. Non-directional: both at one date, the survey date. Not the same as how people were chosen.
Timing: historical, concurrent, mixed
When exposure and outcome happened, relative to the date the study started collecting. Historical: both before. Concurrent: both after. Mixed: one on each side. Most case-control studies are mixed. Often called retrospective and prospective, but those words can also mean directionality.
Sample selection
How people were chosen: by exposure, by outcome or by neither. It usually matches the directionality, but need not.
Follow-up period
The time over which a person is observed after their time origin.
Calendar window
The years the data cover. It is not the same as any one person’s follow-up.
Study start (▼)
The date the team started collecting data. Timing is read against it.
Recall bias
Error in reports about the past that differs by what has happened since, for example after a diagnosis.

Short teaching definitions, not formal ones.
